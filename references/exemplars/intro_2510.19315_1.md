Transformers [cite] are the dominant architecture underlying most modern large language models. A substantial body of recent theoretical work has investigated their expressive power [cite], their trainability and ability to generalize to unseen strings of longer lengths [cite], and the extent to which their behavior can be formally verified [cite]. A key finding of this line of work is that transformers with finite precision---the setting most faithful to real-world hardware---recognize various classes of subregular languages depending on the exact assumptions made [cite].

Subregular languages constitute strict subclasses of the regular languages. For instance, the subregular class of star-free languages are precisely those definable by regular expressions that replace the Kleene star with intersection and complementation. The language $a^*b^*$ is star-free because it can be written as $\overline{\overline{\emptyset} \cdot b \cdot a \cdot\overline{\emptyset}}$, whereas $(aa)^*$ is not star-free [cite]. By contrast, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) can recognize all regular languages under a fixed precision assumption [cite], making them strictly more expressive than transformers as language recognizers. However, the strong empirical performance of transformers invites the question as to whether expressive capacity is the most revealing lens through which to compare architectures.
