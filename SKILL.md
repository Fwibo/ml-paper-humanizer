# ML Paper Humanizer (v1.0)

The AI smell in a paper is a handful of writing actions, not a word list: performing (dressing a conclusion up as an insight), defending (writing the reviewer's rebuttal into the paper), piling (numbers, clauses and citations shovelled into one sentence), restating (heading, first sentence, last sentence and caption all saying one thing), drifting (one object under three names, or an internal name left in the paper). Remove the action and the words come out right.

Two things are hard lines. A claim never outruns its evidence, and a concession costs one clause, never a paragraph of self-defence. Numbers, model and dataset names, defined terms, citations and wording the author asked for are never changed by a revision.

Everything else in this skill is a reader's need, a check for abnormal repetition, or an example, because a rule written as a count, a position or a ban is applied by a model at every opportunity and becomes a mannerism within one round; the originals do each of these things sometimes, never on a schedule.

## What the reader needs

- **Clear in one pass.** One point per sentence, with as many relations as that point needs; the point in the main clause and its context in a short clause before it; the subject next to its verb; what the sentence adds at its end, where the reader pauses. A sentence is too long when it carries more things the reader should remember than it has ends to put them at, and a point is spread too thin when it is chopped into a run of short sentences that the reader must join back together. A statement says what is the case; "not" is for denial and contrast, not for saying something positive the long way round.
- **Oriented.** The reader knows what a paragraph is for before its detail, and why a term is being introduced before its definition. Published authors spend a few words on this freely, with the orienting frames the frames file lists; drafts drop them as filler and lose the reader. A paragraph may open on a topic sentence; inside a paragraph the orienting words ride in the opening clause of the sentence they orient, not as a sentence of their own.
- **Linked.** The reader knows how a sentence relates to the one before it without working the relation out. Most links in published prose are made with no connective at all: the sentence opens on what the reader already holds, the thing the previous sentence ended on or the paragraph's subject, and ends on what it adds. Where that order does not make the relation plain, the field's ordinary frames state it, and the frames file shows what they look like. The same frame in consecutive sentences is the opposite failure.
- **Named.** Every object, mechanism, setting and finding has one name, the author's, used the same way each time it appears. A draft that describes a thing where the paper has a name for it, or coins a new label for a thing the author already named, reads as written by someone outside the work.
- **Grounded.** Readings follow from the problem, the method and the evidence, not from the writer's assertion. A result sentence says what the result means; the surprising thing is marked as surprising and the expected thing as expected; a table is read by the one comparison the argument needs.
- **Plain where it counts.** A technical statement is followed, when it matters, by the same point in plain words ("i.e.", "This means", "Overall"); a term that reappears far from its definition may get a one-clause reminder.
- **Easy to follow.** A paragraph keeps to one topic string: its sentences share a subject, or a small set the reader can hold ("the base model … it … the RL-trained model … both"); a subject that changes with every sentence makes the reader re-find what the paragraph is about. An action is a verb and whoever does it is the subject ("we evaluate X", "the retriever drops Y"), not a nominalization on an empty verb ("the evaluation of X is performed"); the field's nominal names for things (memory extraction, context flow) are names, not actions, and stay. Items that stand in parallel, a list, a comparison, two conditions, take the same grammatical shape: three noun phrases or three clauses, not a noun and then a clause.

## Order of work

Two modes: `rewrite` (the user's text, minimal edits; the default) and `write` (a new passage from an outline, notes or a rough draft). Comments only, or the checker alone, is asked for in the prompt, not a mode. When the user has a skeleton from [ml-paper-outliner](ml-paper-outliner/SKILL.md) (format in [ml-paper-outliner/references/skeleton.md](ml-paper-outliner/references/skeleton.md)), it is the outline for `write` and the fidelity check for `rewrite`; a text whose logic needs sorting out before its wording goes to that skill first.

1. **Orientation and section.** Pick proof / effect / design / evaluation / discovery and read [references/section-playbook.md](references/section-playbook.md), the short index with the orientation tags and the opening logic, and then only the file of the section being written in [references/sections/](references/sections/) (`introduction.md`, `experiments.md`, …): its frame and the lines tagged with the orientation. The other section files are not read. The frame is the things that section does and their usual order; it is kept, the wording is free, and bold, boxes and run-in headings are format that follows the venue template. A different order needs a reason the argument gives (a proof that states the theorem first, an error analysis that opens on a case); without one, the frame stands. With a skeleton, the orientation, the section's Carries and Frame lines and its figure plan come from it, and the playbook is still read for the section's lines: the skeleton carries the argument, the playbook what this field expects of the section's prose (where a paragraph opens, what a caption does, how a concession is sized), which no skeleton writes down. Each numbered note of the skeleton becomes one paragraph, written from the note's instructions, not by paraphrasing them. A note whose evidence is planned is written as planned, and one whose evidence is not identified, or that carries a `[?]`, is left as a marked gap in the text, not written around.
2. **Term ledger.** The names the paper gives to things: objects, mechanisms, quantities, settings, protocols, findings, and the sub-field's standard terms the paper uses as given. Take the author's list when there is one. Otherwise build it from the author's own text (the paper so far, the brief, the notes) and the papers it cites: `python scripts/term_ledger.py <paper .tex/.md/.txt …> [--cited <.tex/.pdf …>] --out ledger.json` lists the statistical candidates (marked or defined at first use, used in a heading or caption, shared with the cited papers, recurring and specific); then read the author's text once yourself and add the names it uses only once or twice (a metaphor in quotes, a protocol the author phrases a certain way, a label given to a finding), each with the sentence it comes from, into `names.json`, and run `python scripts/term_ledger.py --verify names.json ledger.json`, which keeps only what occurs verbatim in the text. The author prunes the result once. A ledger is the author's vocabulary, not a style choice: its names are used verbatim, one name per thing, and no multiword label is coined for a thing the ledger already names. A name covers its other forms: a mechanism's verb is part of its name, used wherever that action recurs, and a synonym for the verb is a second name.
3. **Read the examples for the moves you are about to write**: [references/sentence-bank.md](references/sentence-bank.md), Part A for the failure types and Part B for the moves. In `write` mode also run `python scripts/pick_exemplars.py <intro|results> --orientation <o>` and read the passages (the pool covers introductions and results sections; for other sections the bank and the section frame are the material). Then read [references/connectives.md](references/connectives.md) once, the forms this field uses for cause, consequence, contrast and concession, so that they are at hand while writing. Nothing from these is reused: no content, no run of six or more words.
4. **Write or revise.** How each sentence follows from the one before is decided as it is written. Most sentences need no connective, because their order carries the relation; one is written where the reader would otherwise take the relation wrong, and it stays in the form it was written in. A reporting verb on an inference (suggesting that, indicates, we conclude) is not a connective: a conclusion drawn from the sentence before carries one, and is not joined to its premise by "and". The verb is decided with the sentence too: a mechanism keeps the ledger's verb, and otherwise the verb that came first stays.
5. **Read back** once for fidelity (scope, conditions, negations, what each number modifies, every term recoverable; with a skeleton, also every paragraph note still has its paragraph, no claim added, no claim's verb or scope moved, each paragraph still ending on what its note says and the next picking it up, as [skeleton.md](ml-paper-outliner/references/skeleton.md) says), once for the ledger (each thing under its ledger name, no coined label), and once for the reader's needs above, as someone who reads first sentences and skims. In this read go sentence by sentence on one question: what is the point of the sentence and where does the reader meet it? The point sits in the main clause, its context in a short clause before it, and the subject stays next to its verb; what the sentence adds lands at its end. A point carried by a which-clause behind a main clause that only sets up, or a verb held back by a long subject, is turned around ("Since ⟨setup⟩, ⟨point⟩"; "It remains open which ⟨NP⟩ …"); a sentence that already reads this way keeps whatever shape it has. The rest of this read is at paragraph level. Fix by re-ordering and by adding the missing signpost, gloss, example or reading, and by compressing elsewhere whatever the addition costs, so the passage ends no longer than the exemplar says this content takes. Then read one exemplar passage of the same section type side by side with your draft and ask which one these authors wrote. Where yours spends more words on the same content or walks the reader through a result the exemplar names, revise toward the exemplar; frames are the next step's job.
6. **Frames pass** (in `write` mode always; in `rewrite` mode when the user asks for it). Read [references/register-frames.md](references/register-frames.md) in full, then go through the draft sentence by sentence as a reader of this field and revise sentences in place in one kind, the marking frames below. At each sentence ask whether it applies, and apply it where it does. Connectives and verbs stay as written; the pass adds, moves, replaces or fronts none of them. A background sentence that simply follows the one before it stays as it is.
   - **Marking frames, wherever the function is there.** A sentence that points to a figure, table, section or citation, reports an observation, gives the reading of a result, compares two things, sets the scope of what follows, or orders steps gets the field's frame for that function, from the pointer, result, comparison, scope, sequence, hedge and naming groups: "As shown in ⟨ref⟩,", "We observe that ⟨clause⟩", "This indicates that ⟨clause⟩", "compared with ⟨NP⟩", "For instance, on ⟨benchmark⟩,", "It remains an open question whether ⟨clause⟩". The verb of a result or reading frame follows [references/claim-strength.md](references/claim-strength.md): what the evidence is decides between suggests, indicates, shows and demonstrates, and a pointer frame ("As shown in") does not replace a reporting verb, it precedes one. So does a sentence that states what the authors did as their action ("We adopt ⟨NP⟩ as", "We use ⟨NP⟩", "We conduct ⟨NP⟩", "We present ⟨NP⟩"), the purpose of that action ("To ⟨verb⟩ ⟨NP⟩, we", "in order to", "used to ⟨verb⟩"), or asks for the reader's attention or restates a point in plain words ("Note that ⟨clause⟩", "In other words,", "that is,"), from the method, cause-as-purpose and other groups. These frames assert nothing; they tell the reader what kind of sentence this is, and results and analysis prose in this field carries them on most sentences.
   - **Last look at the connectives, reporting only.** Read each connective between sentences and take it out in your head; list the ones the reader would not miss, and list any junction that reads wrong without one. The pass changes none of them; the writer or the author decides.
7. **Run `python scripts/check_tex.py <tex-or-md> [--ledger ledger.json]`** and look at what it flags. It reports abnormal repetition relative to the document itself (the same opener in consecutive paragraphs, a run of same-length sentences, a device recurring in one section, a term under two names, a word that may carry no meaning, a heading restated by its first and last sentence, a gap or gain whose paragraph names neither its two sides nor a table, a section with several colons and semicolons), and with a ledger the ledger names the draft never uses and the recurring noun phrases the ledger does not have ("coined?"). It has no targets and never says a count is too low or too high. Fix what you agree is a problem and say when a flag is a false alarm. Across the sections of one paper, look also for the same marking frame opening the same position in every section.
8. **Wording pass by another model (optional, only on the user's request).** Some writers prefer another model's English. In a blind test on three sections, a Gemini revision of the checked draft, filtered as below, was judged closer to the published authors than the draft alone, and better written. The step sends the section and the skeleton to an outside service, so it runs only when the user asks for it, with the endpoint the user has set (`HUMANIZER_LLM_BASE_URL`, `HUMANIZER_LLM_API_KEY`, `HUMANIZER_LLM_MODEL`). Run `python scripts/external_draft.py <section> --skeleton skeleton.md --names ledger.json --orientation <o> --revise <draft.md> --out <final.md>`. The other model gets the reader's needs, claim-strength, the bank and the passages, and changes wording only. `scripts/revise_check.py` then puts back every change to a number, citation, reference, math span, heading, bold label or Keep item, writes the text to `<final.md>` and lists the remaining changes in `<final>.changes.md`. Read that list, not the reference files again, and ask one question of each change: does it move a claim's verb or scope, turn an offered cause or reading into a fact or the reverse, add or remove a claim, qualifier, intensifier, example or closing reading, or put a second name on a thing? If it does, put the before text back in `<final.md>`, or repair the sentence so it keeps the before text's claim; otherwise keep it. A phrase the list shows as renamed across the text keeps its original name everywhere. Then run step 7's checker once more. In the tests the other model changed 13 to 28 places and about half were put back. The read-back and the frames pass, run again after this step, changed nothing, so they are not repeated. The step comes after the frames pass, not in its place: the revision improved a draft that had been through step 6, and did not improve one that skipped it. Done in the same session as steps 1 to 7, it added about a tenth to their cost.

## What the read-back looks for

Each item is a failure the bank documents, with the number of the pair that shows it.

- A paragraph that opens on the result or the instrument and never says what the test is for (A1, A2, A7).
- Two or three points in one sentence, often as appositives and which-clauses (A8, A10, A12); one point spread over a run of short sentences; a statement made as a denial where the reader wants to know what is.
- A precise but strange phrase where the field has a stock one (A14, A15, A16), never applied to the paper's own terms, which are the ledger's and stay however unusual they sound.
- A thing described where the ledger names it; a new multiword label for a thing the ledger already names; one object under two names.
- A term defined before the reader knows why it matters (A20, A21, A23).
- A number or curve reported without its reading, or a surprise left unmarked (A26, A28, A30).
- A result or comparison sentence with no frame; an inference joined to its premise by "and" instead of carrying its reporting verb; a connective that can be removed without the reader losing a step, or one re-cast from the form the writer gave it (so → Since, yet → Although); a bridging sentence whose subject has no antecedent ("Two cases do not settle the question"); a gap, gain or margin whose two sides, metric and table the paragraph never names.
- A point made once in technical form and never in plain words; a paragraph ending on its last detail instead of its point (A31, A32, A35).
- A table read cell by cell instead of by the comparison that matters (A36, A37, A39); a caption that argues the result instead of saying what the table or figure shows.
- A paragraph whose subject changes with every sentence; an action hidden in a nominalization on an empty verb; a list or comparison whose items differ in grammatical shape.
- Hype words standing in for a claim, a pre-emptive rebuttal, a heading restated by the first and last sentence, an announcing sentence that carries nothing, a reading framed as the authors' verdict ("We take this as") instead of carried by the evidence.

## Output contract

- `rewrite`: minimal edits, the author's wording kept where it reads in one pass; one layer at a time, structure before sentences before words; a defined term is named once and then used bare, not re-glossed at each appearance; revised text in full, then a change list ("before → after, reason"). Rewrite whole paragraphs, or run the frames pass over the text, only when asked. Flag doubtful numbers or facts instead of correcting them.
- `write`: follow the section's frame, read the bank and the passages first, use the ledger's names verbatim, run the read-back, the frames pass and the checker, give the pass's change list ("before → after, which marking frame"), the connectives the last look questioned, a line confirming that the pass added, moved, removed or re-cast no connective and changed no verb, and a list of any thing the text names that the ledger lacks ("not in ledger: …") for the author to add or rename. With step 8, also the model that revised the wording and, for each change on its list, kept or put back, with the reason for each one put back.

When the user wants a Chinese version alongside, translate clause by clause, keep terms and claim nouns in English, map connectives one to one, and add no causality.

## Reference files

- [references/sentence-bank.md](references/sentence-bank.md): the primary teaching material; 40 draft-vs-authors pairs by failure type and 23 further sentences by move, from award papers.
- [references/exemplars/](references/exemplars/): 144 passages from 30 award and reference papers, introductions (64) and results sections (80), drawn by `scripts/pick_exemplars.py`; sources and licenses in `SOURCES.md`.
- [references/register-frames.md](references/register-frames.md): the stock frames of published ML papers by sentence function (pointer, result, comparison, purpose, hedge, scope, sequence, naming, method), counted over 307 award and oral papers; read in the frames pass (step 6) only. Reading it before writing was tested and changed nothing.
- [references/connectives.md](references/connectives.md): the cause, consequence, contrast and concession connectives from the same count; read once before writing (step 3), never applied by the pass.
- [references/section-playbook.md](references/section-playbook.md): the index of the section files, with the core principle, the orientation tags and the opening logic per orientation; one file per section in [references/sections/](references/sections/), each a full guide to that section: what it does for the reader and the reviewer, its frame with the reason for the order, its rules with reasons, examples and the guide each comes from, what it must do per orientation, the common mistakes, and a checklist for the finished section; the guides behind them in `sections/sources.md`. Read the index and the one section file.
- [references/claim-strength.md](references/claim-strength.md): the honesty line, neither defensive nor overclaiming, the reporting verbs by what the evidence is, and design intent kept apart from outcome.
- [ml-paper-outliner/](ml-paper-outliner/SKILL.md): the companion skill that writes and checks the argument skeleton (contribution, orientation, a brief per section with one note per paragraph: question, claim at its strength, evidence, what it does not say, hand-off) before the prose, or recovers it from a draft as a reverse outline; it shares the playbook and claim-strength with this skill.
- `scripts/term_ledger.py`: the term ledger's statistical candidates and the verbatim check of the names a model adds (needs spaCy `en_core_web_sm` for the candidates; `--verify` runs without it).
- `scripts/check_tex.py`: the anomaly report of step 7; plain Python, no dependencies.
- `scripts/external_draft.py`: step 8's call to another model through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, with its prompt cut from this skill's own files; it can also draft from a skeleton, which tested below this skill's own draft. Plain Python, no dependencies.
- `scripts/revise_check.py`: step 8's comparison of the checked draft with the revision; it puts back what a script can judge and lists the rest. Plain Python, no dependencies.
