# Sources of the section files

The frames restate, per section, what these guides ask for; none is reproduced. The skill is for AI conference papers (ICLR, NeurIPS, ICML, ACL, CVPR and their kind), so the guides written for that field are the primary sources, and a rule stands on them alone. Guides written for other fields are secondary: a line taken from one of them stands only where a primary guide or the exemplar passages of award papers agree with it, and is dropped where the field's practice differs.

Primary (AI conferences). The Contribution-Centered Paper Writing Checklist (LARS Group, 2026) gives the core principle, the five orientations, the Figure 1 and Table 1 plans and the section checklists for Title, Abstract, Introduction, Related Work, Method and Experiments; it has no Conclusion section. Black, *Writing a good scientific paper* (MPI-IS, 2024; CVPR, ICCV, NeurIPS, SIGGRAPH), gives goal–problem–solution as the abstract's and the introduction's rhythm, the insight ("nugget") apart from the technical contribution, the related-work paragraph that says what prior art got right and what it missed, the simplest baseline first and one change at a time, equations that match the code, the concept in three ways, the caption that says what to see, and the conclusion as a present-tense abstract that reads the results a step further with limitations phrased as a path forward. Freeman, *How to write a good CVPR submission* (MIT, 2014), quoting Kajiya, gives the toy example before the algorithm, experimental results as the thing CVPR now demands, and the ending on conclusions or on what the work opens up rather than on a future-work list. 刘知远《如何写一篇合格的NLP论文》(2021; read in a reader's summary) gives the correspondence of challenges, ideas, components and experiments, the method written whole to parts with the introduction's account detailed rather than repeated, the main experiment as a controlled test of the paper's own claim that need not beat the field, the auxiliary experiments each serving the contribution, the caption a reviewer can read without the body, and the two positions of related work. Nanda, *Highly Opinionated Advice on How to Write ML Papers* (2025), gives one to three claims scoped by their evidence, baselines tuned as hard as the method, one decisive experiment over several similar ones, evidence of different kinds, the cherry-picking level of an example, and pre-specified against post-hoc analyses. Lipton, *Heuristics for Scientific Writing (a Machine Learning Perspective)* (2018), gives section balance, the contribution reached early, the reviewer's question answered before it is asked and intensifiers removed. The NeurIPS Paper Checklist gives the claims that match the results and their scope, and the limitations section on assumptions, robustness and scope. Karpathy, *A Survival Guide to a PhD* (2016), gives the one-contribution rule, the experiments that answer the introduction's challenges, and the ban on steps listed without their reasons. Lipton & Steinhardt, *Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship* (2018), is saved with the others and not yet drawn on.

Secondary (other fields). Mensh & Kording, *Ten simple rules for structuring papers* (2017), for the results paragraph that opens on its question and closes on its answer and the subsection headed by a declarative sentence, both of which the discovery exemplars show. Ernst, *How to write a technical paper* (2005, rev. 2024), for the method stated as output and key idea before steps, the successful approach before any alternative, the running example (also Freeman), the related-work section the reader can summarise (also Black), and the rules on digits and on relative against absolute numbers. Widom, *Tips for Writing Technical Papers*, for the introduction's five questions, which the checklist's introduction frame also asks. Peyton Jones, *How to write a great research paper*, for the refutable contribution list, which Nanda's claims rule also asks. Swales, the CARS model, for the introduction moves used as tags in the outliner's reverse mode. Whitesides, *Writing a Paper* (2004), for the outline written before the text, which Black and Nanda also ask. Ernst's rule that tables and figures share one numbering, Mensh & Kording's discussion structure and Peyton Jones's related work at the end are not followed: the field does otherwise.

Not every line has a guide behind it. The untagged lines on displayed equations, parentheses, the rebuttal, the conclusion's wording rule on conjectures, and the method's ordering rule where data flow and bottlenecks disagree were written from the exemplar passages of award papers and from feedback on drafts. They are practice defaults, kept apart here so that a reader can tell a sourced rule from one the skill's authors settled on.
