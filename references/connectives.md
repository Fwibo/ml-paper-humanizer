# Connectives of published ML papers

The cause, consequence, contrast and concession connectives from the same count as [register-frames.md](register-frames.md): 307 award and oral papers, each frame in at least 6 of them, `papers` = how many papers use it, ★ = used in published passages and rarely in model drafts of the same passages.

How to use. Read once before writing (SKILL.md step 3). A connective is decided with the sentence it belongs to and keeps the form it was written in.

## What the writing guides say

1. **Order first.** Readers expect the opening of a sentence, the topic position, to give "linkage (looking backward) and context (looking forward)", and its end, the stress position, to carry the new information they should emphasize (Gopen and Swan 1990; Williams's old before new). Most relations between sentences are carried by this order alone.
2. **Mark a turn early.** A change of direction (but, yet, however, nevertheless) is signalled as soon as possible, so the reader takes the first half of the sentence in the right direction (Zinsser's mood changers). Feak and Swales (1994) report that a sentence connector in initial position signals something contrary to expectation, while in medial position it marks a contrast that is not unexpected.
3. **Three kinds, three shapes.** Subordinators (although, because, since, while) fold two clauses into one sentence; coordinators (but, so, yet, and) join two clauses with a comma; sentence connectors (However, Therefore, In contrast, For example) open or sit inside a new sentence between commas (Swales and Feak's categories). Each gives the sentence a different shape and emphasis; choose by the shape the sentence should have.
4. **The reader's test, in both directions.** A writer who already holds the relation drops the connective the reader needs (Pinker's curse of knowledge); a run of sentences each joined by and, but or so reads monotonous (Strunk's rule 14). A connective is written where the relation would otherwise be taken wrong, and left out where the order already carries it.
5. **Repeat the word, vary the shape.** Fowler: "Variation should take place only when there is some awkwardness, such as ambiguity or noticeable monotony, in the word avoided", and elegant variation "is generally a worse fault than monotony"; Williams likewise prefers repeating the word, since two words suggest two things. What reads mechanical is a run of sentences in one shape; that is varied by shape, a subordinate clause, a coordinate clause or no connective. Parallel content keeps parallel form (Strunk's rule 15).

Gopen and Swan close their list with "None of these reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules'"; the same holds here.
